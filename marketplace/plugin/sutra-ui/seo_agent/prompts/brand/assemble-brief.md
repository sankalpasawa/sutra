## Resolving the places they disagree

The material above came from documents written at different times by different people, so parts of it
contradict other parts. **You resolve those. Do not print both sides, and do not hedge.** When two rules
cannot both be followed, pick one and write only the winner into the brief.

Pick using these, in order:

1. **A legal or safety line always wins.** A rule that exists to keep a claim defensible beats a rule
   that exists to make copy punchier.
2. **A rule beats a sample.** Brand documents quote real published copy as examples, and published copy
   often breaks rules written after it shipped. When a quoted sample uses a banned construction, argues
   the opposite of a stated position, or models something the rules forbid, **keep the rule and drop the
   sample.** Do not keep a sample and annotate it as an exception.
3. **A closed named list beats an open category.** "Never cite these nine named companies" is usable
   while writing; "never cite a competitor" forces the writer to adjudicate mid-sentence.
4. **The narrower rule wins when the broader one would make ordinary writing impossible.**
5. **A default beats an exception** when the exception adds ambiguity without adding real value.
6. **Only when none of the above settles it**, keep both and state the boundary between them in one line.
   This should be rare. Two rules left facing each other in the brief is the failure this file exists to
   end.

## How to fill it

- **Move the content, do not reinvent it.** Every concrete item above appears in your output: every word
  pair, every banned phrase, every house spelling, every named competitor. If the material gave you 23
  industry terms, all 23 come through. Losing a specific is the one failure mode that matters, because a
  specific is the only thing a writer can actually act on.
- **Tighten the prose around the specifics.** The source documents explain themselves at length. Cut the
  explanation, keep the instruction. A paragraph reading "Durability is a core promise, stated in
  carefully bounded language: the coating resists scratching and the finish is warranted for ten years"
  becomes "Write *resists scratching*, never *scratch-proof*. Test: could a lawyer sign off on this
  sentence?"
- **Keep a rule's own test or check when it has one.** Those are the most useful lines in the pack.
- **Use tables for anything that is a pairing or a list.** A writer scans a table; it reads a paragraph.
- **Nothing here is general writing craft.** If a line would be equally true for a shoe company, it does
  not belong in this file. Sentence length, banned clichés, number formatting, punctuation: leave all of
  it out, even where it appeared in the material above.
- **Nothing here is a fact.** No statistic, no customer story, no product number. Facts are handed to the
  writer separately, already carrying their sources.
- **Never invent.** If the template has a section and the material gave you nothing for it, write
  `*(nothing recorded)*` under that heading rather than filling it from your own knowledge of the company.
- **Length follows the material.** Do not pad, and do not compress past the point where a specific is lost.
