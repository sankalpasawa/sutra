THE RHYTHM, AND THIS IS THE FAULT THE LAST RUN ACTUALLY HAD

A LOW AVERAGE IS NOT THE GOAL. VARIETY IS.

This has now gone wrong twice, in two different ways, so read this part twice.

The first time, a run took an article's average from 14.9 to 10.8 and it read worse, not better,
because it levelled every sentence into the same 8-to-14-word band. Sentences over 20 words went from
24% of the article to 1%. The longest dropped from 32 words to 25. Nothing was wrong with any single
sentence; the article had lost its music, and a page with no long sentence in it reads like a form.

The second time was worse, because the brief caused it. The brief said "roughly one sentence in five
should run 18 words or more", and that is a FIXED target. Handed an article where half the sentences
already ran long, the model cut its way DOWN to one in five and destroyed exactly the variety it was
supposed to protect. A fixed target is a ceiling as often as it is a floor. So the number below is
computed from this block, and it is a FLOOR.

So, in every block you hand back:

  · KEEP YOUR LONGEST SENTENCE LONG. If the block you were given contains a sentence over 20 words,
    the block you hand back contains one too. Split the three-clause sentences; leave the one long
    sentence that carries a single idea well.
  · THIS BLOCK'S OWN TARGET, AND IT IS COUNTED. Of its {{SENT_NOW}} sentences, {{LONG_NOW}} run 18
    words or more. HAND BACK AT LEAST {{LONG_KEEP}} SENTENCES OF 18 WORDS OR MORE. Count them before
    you reply. This number is not a general rule of thumb, it is this block's own current variety,
    and keeping it is the difference between splitting the sentences that needed it and levelling
    every sentence in the block.
  · NEVER LET EVERY SENTENCE IN A PARAGRAPH LAND IN THE SAME BAND. Short, short, long. Long, short.
    A four-word sentence after a twenty-two-word one lands hard, and that is the whole effect.
  · THREE SHORT SENTENCES IN A ROW IS A DELIBERATE DEVICE, USED ONCE OR TWICE IN A BLOCK. Four in a
    row is a stutter, and on a subject the reader came here to learn it reads as talking down to them.

The average will come down on its own once the three-clause sentences are split. Chasing the average
by shortening sentences that were already fine is how the last run went wrong.

NEVER WEAKEN A CLAIM TO MAKE IT FIT

Splitting a sentence can quietly change what it says, and code cannot see it. Two real examples from
the last run, both of which passed every automated check:

   GIVEN:  "Organizations that put a validated assessment in front of that step cut turnover by an
            average of 65% against teams relying on resumes and interviews alone."
   HANDED BACK: "Some organizations put a validated assessment in front of that step. They cut
            turnover by an average of 65% ..."
   WRONG. "Some organizations" is a different, weaker claim than "organizations that do X". The
   research says what happens when you do the thing. The rewrite says some people did the thing.

   GIVEN:  "Adverse impact is what happens when one selection standard, applied identically to
            everybody, produces substantially different pass rates across demographic groups."
   HANDED BACK: "Adverse impact is what happens when one selection standard produces substantially
            different pass rates across demographic groups. You applied that standard identically to
            everybody."
   WRONG. "Applied identically to everybody" is part of the DEFINITION — it is the whole point, that
   a fair-looking rule can still do this. Moved into its own sentence it becomes an assertion about
   what the reader did, and the definition is now incomplete.

So:

  · NEVER ADD A QUANTIFIER OR A HEDGE that was not there: some, many, often, usually, generally,
    sometimes, might, may, tends to. A sourced claim is stated as flatly in your version as in the
    one you were given.
  · A DEFINITION IS ONE SENTENCE. When a sentence defines a term, its qualifying clauses are part of
    the definition. Re-shape around them, never lift them out into a sentence of their own.
  · NO FILLER WORDS TO SMOOTH A JOIN. The last run produced "Turnover is what moves here", "Use a
    different set of questions too", and "Scores then build your shortlist. They no longer thin it
    three weeks later." Each is a word or a clause added to paper over a split. Split it cleanly or
    leave the sentence alone.

THE FIVE MOVES

These are the only edits you make. Every one of them is a real before-and-after from this brand's
own articles.

1. THE THREE-CLAUSE SENTENCE, SPLIT INTO THREE.
   The commonest fault by far. A subject, an action and a consequence stacked into one sentence.

   BEFORE: "You pick the skills assessment, send it to forty applicants, and by Friday you are
            staring at a column of numbers that decides who gets a phone call."
   AFTER:  "You pick a skills assessment. You send it to forty applicants. By Friday you are looking
            at a column of numbers, and those numbers decide who gets a phone call."

2. THE COLON IN THE MIDDLE, BROKEN OUT.
   A colon mid-sentence makes the reader hold the first half while parsing the second.

   BEFORE: "Completion holds near 80% up to about forty minutes, then falls to around 60% once total
            testing runs past an hour."
   AFTER:  "Completion holds near 80% up to about forty minutes. Past an hour of total testing, it
            falls to around 60%."

3. THE TRAILING LIST GLUED TO A MAIN CLAUSE, CUT INTO FRAGMENTS.
   People list things out loud in fragments. Write them that way.

   BEFORE: "Looking away to think, a housemate crossing the frame, a connection dropping for eight
            seconds: all of it trips the same wire."
   AFTER:  "Looking away to think. A housemate crossing the frame. A connection dropping for eight
            seconds. All of it trips the same wire."

4. THE INVERTED OR PASSIVE OPENING, TURNED BACK TO SUBJECT-VERB.
   Say who does the thing, then what they do.

   BEFORE: "A cut score is the line that splits pass from fail. It is set by a person rather than
            discovered in the data."
   AFTER:  "A cut score is the line that splits pass from fail. A person sets it."

5. THE FORMAL WORD, SWAPPED FOR THE WORKING ONE.
   instrument becomes test. abandons becomes quits. outscored becomes beat. establishes it by
   becomes proves it by. in accordance with becomes in line with. utilise becomes use.

SIMPLE TO UNDERSTAND, WHICH IS NOT THE SAME AS SHORT

A reader should finish a sentence knowing what it meant, first time, without going back.

  · Use the word a working {{READER}} would use out loud. Not the word an academic paper would use.
  · Say who is doing the thing. A person, a team, a company, and the reader is "you". Never let a
    thing do what only a person can. Not "the cost scales with seniority" but "you pay more for
    senior roles".
  · One idea per sentence. When a sentence has an "and" joining two complete thoughts, it is two
    sentences wearing one coat.
  · Put the point first, the qualification after. Not "Unless a test has been validated for your
    specific job, it is not valid for that job" if the point survives being led with.
  · When you meet a number, make sure the sentence says what it MEANS, not only what it is. If the
    original already does that, leave it alone.

THE TERMS OF ART STAY. Every single one.
This article exists to teach words like validity, norm group, cut score, adverse impact, percentile
rank, reliability coefficient. Never swap one for a plain-English paraphrase. Never delete one.
A reader came here to learn that word.

What you MAY do is make the sentence AROUND the term carry its plain meaning, when that costs you no
words. "Validity is not a property the test owns" becomes "Validity is not something the test owns".
Same term, same length, one less thing to decode.

LONG WORDS ARE NOT THE PROBLEM AND NEVER WERE. Say standardized, conventional, demographic,
predictive. Plainness comes from short IDEAS per sentence, never from short words. An article of
one-syllable words in three-clause sentences is still unreadable.
