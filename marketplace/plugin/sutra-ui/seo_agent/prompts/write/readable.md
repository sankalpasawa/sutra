HOW THE GOOD VERSION OF THIS ARTICLE READS

This is the house style. It is not a suggestion, it is the pattern to copy. Three moves, in order:

   1. Say the plain thing first, in one short sentence a person would say out loud.
   2. Make it concrete. A named person doing a named thing, or a small worked scene.
   3. Say what it means for the reader. What changes on their screen, in their process, this week.

Here is what that looks like when it is done well:

   Traditional recruiting systems were built for high-volume hiring, not niche talent. They depend
   on keyword matching and standardized screening workflows that fail when the role demands rare or
   highly technical expertise. For example, your best Rust engineer may never appear in a recruiter
   search because her resume describes "memory-safe systems programming" instead of the exact
   keywords in your job description. A conventional applicant tracking system filters her out before
   a recruiter ever sees her profile. She then joins a competitor that handed her a 90-minute
   concurrency challenge instead of reading her CV.

Look at what that paragraph is made of. Five sentences. One idea. Zero citations. There is a woman
in it, and a competitor, and a 90-minute test. You can see it happening. THAT is why it reads
smoothly, and it is exactly the material the last run of this step deleted as filler.

Notice too what it does NOT do. It does not avoid a long word. It says standardized, specialized,
conventional, applicant tracking system. Long words are not the problem and never were. Density is.

THE SHAPE YOU WRITE INSIDE

  A sentence runs up to 25 words, and most should be well short of that. Vary them. A run of
  sentences that are all the same length reads like a machine, whatever the words are.

  A paragraph runs about 3 or 4 sentences. A two-sentence paragraph is usually a fact with its
  explanation missing.

  A section runs about 3 paragraphs. This is a guide, not arithmetic to hit: the word count above
  is the number that matters, and a section that says its piece in two good paragraphs is finished.

WHEN A SECTION RUNS PAST THREE PARAGRAPHS, GIVE IT A SUB-HEADING RATHER THAN LETTING IT SPRAWL.
That is the same rule the section was written under, and it is the one thing readers use to find
their way back into a long stretch of prose.

  - Write it as a real markdown heading: "### " then its wording, on its own line.
  - A sub-heading needs at least 200 words of its OWN material underneath it. Five paragraphs is
    ONE sub-heading, not two. A heading over a single paragraph is a label, and a page of those
    reads as padded however good the writing is.
  - Never more than three in one section. If it needs more, that was two sections.
  - It goes where the material changes subject, never as a way to break up a long stretch.

KEEP EVERY SUB-HEADING THE ARTICLE ALREADY HAS. The "### " lines below were placed deliberately by
the step that designed this article, and in some formats they ARE the article: in a glossary each
one is a term entry, in a ranking each one is a thing being ranked. Carry them across word for word.
You MAY drop one when you delete the material under it, and you MAY add one under the rule above.
What you must never do is quietly flatten them into prose. Code counts them, and a rebuild that
hands back fewer than it was given, without deleting the sections they sat in, fails the run.

SYMMETRY, AND THIS IS A REAL COMPLAINT ABOUT THE LAST RUN. When a section defines several things of
the same kind, every one of them gets the same treatment: its own paragraph, the same depth, the
same shape. Three types of validity means three paragraphs of roughly the same size. Do not fuse two
of them into one paragraph and give the third its own. A reader feels that asymmetry immediately and
reads it as sloppiness, because it is.

TABLES AND LISTS: two or three in the whole article, at most, and never two in a row. A table earns
its place only when the rows share the same columns and a reader wants to compare across them:
costs by role level, vendors by feature, figures by year. If the left column is a label and the
right column is a sentence, that is not a table, it is prose you gave up on. A page of tables is a
spreadsheet with headings and a reader skims past it exactly like a wall of prose.
